RESEARCH NOTE · 13 DATED ENTRIES · 9 PUBLIC SOURCES

Upwork’s policy shifts:
from relationship fees to paid access

Annotated chronology and short narrative for Paulo H. Leocadio’s Navigating Upwork: Understanding Corporate Dynamics and Unveiling Freelancer Realities Amid Policy Disruption, DOI 10.2139/ssrn.6031034.

Prepared 28 October 2026 · Historical scope: 2013 corporate prehistory to 15 July 2024; central policy window 2016–2024. This is not a description of current Upwork terms.

Main finding. The paper’s 20%/10%/5% fee schedule is historical, not the general marketplace schedule at its internal July 2024 date. The May 2023 change to 10%, the end-2023 transition, and increased proposal costs should be separated before interpreting fee-related fairness findings. [S6–S8]

How the paper determines the scope

The supplied 38-page PDF carries a July 15, 2024 date and ©2024, although the task citation says 2026. Its references include July 2024 retrievals. I therefore use July 15, 2024 as the manuscript-content cutoff; a 2026 deposit or version history is not independently established here.

The paper describes a systematic review with independent duplicate screening/extraction/coding/risk-of-bias assessment, reporting 218 studies/reports and 495 perspectives for its first observation, and 50 sources for policy-enforcement equality (pp.2–3). These are author-reported counts, not a dataset reproduced by this reconstruction. Their overlap is unclear, so they are not summed. Section 11 adds the author’s personal account: joining around 2015/16, seven years of experience, agency expansion, an account compromise and an adverse platform response (pp.34–35). The six-month research journey has no exact start/end dates.

The paper contains no auditable event-level suspension corpus or policy-version register; p.36 says no user data were used beyond public citations. Accordingly, this reconstruction is a focused documentary history of fees, proposal access and governance, not a causal study or verification of the individual case narratives. Reported proposal success around 10% is not treated as a platform-wide measured rate.

Short narrative

Upwork’s early pricing logic rewarded accumulated client relationships. In 2016, the move from a flat 10% fee to marginal 20%/10%/5% tiers made small new engagements more expensive while reducing fees on large repeat relationships. That design matters to this paper because its personal account describes an experienced professional building a platform business, whereas other passages emphasize short jobs and repeated applications. Those users did not face the same marginal fee. [S1–S2]

The next shifts concerned access to work and control over interactions. Paid Connects in 2019 placed a cost on applications even when no contract resulted. Upwork’s stated justification was to reduce proposal congestion; contemporaneous reporting records objections from freelancers concerned about entry costs. In June 2020, the reported pre-contract communication restriction extended platform control into interviews. By 2022, proposal boosting made visibility itself purchasable through additional Connects. These changes help explain why application volume and enforcement boundaries belong in the same historical account, but do not establish that boosting reduced hiring chances for any particular group. [S3–S5]

The 2023 changes altered the distribution of costs again. A flat 10% marketplace fee reduced the initial marginal commission for affected low-billing relationships, while the end of temporary 5% treatment raised marginal charges on qualifying established relationships. More Connects were required to bid, and clients acquired a contract-initiation charge. Calling this simply a fee reduction or a fee increase conceals who paid more and at what stage: before hiring, when starting a contract, or on earnings. The paper’s fairness analysis would be better anchored by classifying experiences by policy period and relationship size. [S6–S8]

Enforcement requires a different evidentiary standard. SEC-hosted filings document Upwork’s own descriptions of automated screening, identity checks, fraud controls and disputes; they do not show whether these tools were applied equally. The 2024 U.S. classification rule supplies regulatory context, not a cause of the fee changes or a verdict on freelancer suspensions. The defensible conclusion is that commercial rules changed while enforcement outcomes remain insufficiently observable in this public-record sample. [S1, S7, S9]

Annotated timeline

2013-12 / 2014-03

Corporate foundation

Actors: Elance; oDesk; Elance-oDesk

Incorporated in December 2013 in connection with the combination; renamed Elance-oDesk in March 2014.

Why it matters: Separates the corporate combination from the later Upwork brand.

Paper link: p.6 and p.14 date the merger to 2015; distinguish combination, rebrand and consolidation.
Evidence: High: corporate filings. [S1] [S7]

2015-05 / 2016

Rebrand and consolidation

Actors: Upwork Inc.; Elance and oDesk users

Upwork name adopted May 2015; single marketplace followed platform consolidation in 2016.

Why it matters: Platform governance became concentrated in one marketplace. This is context, not proof of a specific enforcement change.

Paper link: p.34: author joined around 2015/16.
Evidence: High: corporate filings. [S1] [S7]

2016-06

Fee regime change

Actors: Upwork; CEO Stephane Kasriel; freelancers; clients

Replaced flat 10% freelancer fees with marginal client-lifetime tiers: 20% on first $500, 10% on next $9,500, 5% above $10,000. Clients faced 2.75% per payment or $25/month if eligible.

Why it matters: Raised the initial relationship cost while rewarding large repeat relationships; stated rationale was longer-term engagement.

Paper link: pp.2 and 21 describe this historical schedule.
Evidence: High for tiers and quarter/month; exact effective day not established. [S1] [S2]

2018-09-06

Documented governance baseline

Actors: Upwork; Stephane Kasriel; marketplace users

IPO registration disclosed machine-learning admission/matching, identity verification, billing validation and public/private feedback. It identified circumvention and fraud as business risks.

Why it matters: Shows algorithmic gatekeeping and revenue-protection concerns predate later paid visibility products.

Paper link: Sections 5–6 and p.35 discuss enforcement, identity misuse and billing manipulation.
Evidence: High: directly inspected filing passages; no launch date inferred. [S1]

2019-04-02; rollout 2019-05 to 2019-06

Paid access to proposals

Actors: Upwork; VP of Product Jessica Tiwari; freelancers; clients

Announced paid Connects at $0.15 each, with 1–6 tokens per application under the announced design, replacing the Basic plan’s 60 free monthly Connects. Invited proposals had no submission cost.

Why it matters: Applied a monetary price to unsuccessful applications; Upwork argued fewer proposals would help matching and denied a revenue motive.

Paper link: pp.3 and 34–35 describe dozens of applications and a proposal-machine burden.
Evidence: High for reported terms and window; contemporaneous press with named company responses. [S3]

2020-06-26

Pre-contract communication restriction

Actors: Upwork Trust & Safety; Daryl Sando (named in secondary report); clients; freelancers

Version 5.0, effective June 26, 2020, added section 7.2 requiring pre-contract communication on Upwork for Basic/Plus client interactions and prohibiting sharing or seeking direct contact details. Enterprise-client interactions were exempt; the definition included Upwork Business Clients displaying the Enterprise badge. Violations could lead to permanent suspension.

Why it matters: Expanded the compliance perimeter from payments to interviews; exceptions mean rules were not identical across client tiers.

Paper link: pp.22–24 discuss enforcement, circumvention and perceived unequal treatment.
Evidence: Verified primary text: April 3 version 4.5 and June 26 version 5.0 recovered from June 2 and June 27, 2020 Wayback captures and linked original-host PDFs; recovery completed October 29, 2026. [S4]

2022

Paid visibility

Actors: Upwork product team; freelancers; clients

2022 product roundup described bidding extra Connects for the top three proposal positions and an Available Now badge.

Why it matters: Added optional paid visibility on top of the cost of submitting proposals. Do not substitute today’s four-slot mechanics for the historical three-slot account.

Paper link: Proposal competition, pp.3 and 34–35; algorithmic matching, p.13.
Evidence: Moderate: indexed primary product account; exact launch dates not verified. [S5]

2023-01 to 2023-06

Connects requirement increase

Actors: Upwork management/product teams; freelancers

Company disclosed an increase in the number of Connects needed to bid during the first half of 2023.

Why it matters: At a fixed token price, requiring more tokens raises application cost; job-level variation is not reconstructed here.

Paper link: Gives a dated policy context to the paper’s high-volume proposal discussion.
Evidence: Moderate: indexed primary filing excerpt; no uniform per-job token count asserted. [S8]

2023-04

Client contract initiation fee

Actors: Upwork; clients; freelancers indirectly

Introduced a once-per-contract client initiation fee, described in the Q1 letter as up to $4.95, assessed on first payment to talent.

Why it matters: Added a client-side cost of starting an engagement. Any effect on hiring volumes remains a hypothesis.

Paper link: Sections 5.2–5.5 compare freelancer and hiring-company fairness.
Evidence: Moderate-high: convergent indexed primary disclosures. [S6] [S8]

2023-05-03

Flat freelancer service fee

Actors: Upwork management under CEO Hayden Brown; talent; clients

Retired tiered marketplace service fees for flat 10% on new contracts and existing 20% contracts. Existing 5% contracts retained 5% through end-2023.

Why it matters: Lowered the starting marginal rate for affected small relationships but removed the 5% long-relationship advantage after transition.

Paper link: Directly qualifies pp.2, 15 and 21, which present the old schedule without a policy-period distinction.
Evidence: High for change and transition; exact day supported by indexed company letter. [S6] [S7]

2023-12-31 / 2024-01-01

End of 5% transition

Actors: Upwork; freelancers with qualifying existing 5% contracts

Announced temporary retention of 5% ended with 2023; under the announced schedule these relationships moved to 10% in 2024.

Why it matters: For affected future billings, the marginal platform fee doubled from 5% to 10%, not the cumulative lifetime fee. Contract-specific exceptions are not audited.

Paper link: Important for the author’s experienced/high-earner perspective and July 2024 manuscript date.
Evidence: High for announced endpoint; implementation inferred from schedule, not invoice inspection. [S6] [S8]

2024-01-09; effective 2024-03-11

External regulatory context

Actors: U.S. Department of Labor; Acting Secretary Julie Su; Wage and Hour Division

Announced six-factor economic-reality analysis for FLSA employee/contractor status, rescinding the 2021 rule; effective March 11.

Why it matters: Relevant to classification risk, not proof Upwork freelancers became employees or that Upwork changed its suspension rules.

Paper link: p.31 identifies regulation as a risk; keep separate from platform decisions.
Evidence: High: official historical agency release; no present-day legal advice. [S9]

2024-02-15

Documented enforcement and security baseline

Actors: Upwork trust/safety and security teams; third-party verification and fraud vendors; California DFPI

FY2023 filing described machine learning and AI for identity/security checks, pattern-matching for unusual behavior, third-party verification, optional two-factor authentication, escrow and dispute processes.

Why it matters: Confirms enforcement infrastructure and payment-regulation setting; supplies neither suspension rates nor comparative appeal outcomes.

Paper link: pp.22–25 and author’s account-compromise narrative, p.34.
Evidence: High for company’s disclosed infrastructure; not independent validation of effectiveness or individual decisions. [S7]

What this changes in the paper’s interpretation

Paper passageHistorically grounded treatment
20%/10%/5% presented as the fee structure (pp.2, 15, 21)Label as the 2016–2023 tiered regime and distinguish the 2023 transition from the 2024 flat-fee position. Older user experiences may remain valid; do not retrospectively reprice them.
Merger dated to 2015 (pp.6, 14)Separate 2013 incorporation/2014 combination from May 2015 rebranding and 2016 marketplace consolidation.
Arbitrary enforcement, account compromise and unequal treatment (pp.22–25, 34–35)Retain as attributed experiences or claims. Neither the policy text nor corporate security descriptions establish a case outcome or comparative enforcement rate.

Method and limits

This rapid, purposive public-record reconstruction prioritizes records that directly explain the paper’s experiences. It uses SEC corporate filings, company disclosures, contemporaneous press/practitioner accounts and one official agency release. Each entry distinguishes an effective date from a disclosure date or a rollout window. Interpretations of freelancer consequences are separated from the documented decision. All web records were consulted on October 28, 2026.

No legislative, standards-body or patent event is used to explain a platform change without evidence linking it to that change. The paper’s blockchain discussion is prospective, not documentation of a deployment. The 10-K’s security-certification and patent descriptions are not a history of suspension-policy decisions. A broad patent/standards sweep was therefore omitted rather than padded into the chronology. No synthetic observations, fabricated interviews or sample user data are included.

Some historical Upwork pages could be located only in search-index excerpts. These are explicitly marked, not represented as downloaded archives or verified full-text versions. Update, October 29, 2026: the June 2020 entry is now verified against recovered versions 4.5 and 5.0, with dated Wayback captures and linked PDFs. See the recovery note for quotations and the before/after comparison. The earlier agreement already prohibited unsolicited external contact; the new section extended the rule to pre-contract communications generally for covered users. Present-day support pages were not used to backdate current rules. This is a selected chronology, not an exhaustive record of all policy changes through July 2024. No later fee regime is projected backward into the paper.

The author’s account compromise and three illustrative cases on p.23 have no independently verified decision date or case identifier in the supplied text; they are not inserted as dated public events. Assessing unequal enforcement would require incident notices, the applicable agreement version and comparable client/freelancer appeal outcomes.
