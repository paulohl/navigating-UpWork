M2 · Add the dated communication-rule change

Placement: New §5.1.5 after §5.1.4 and before §5.2 on p.22. This supplies the primary-text example missing from the current policy overview.

Existing section anchor: “Upwork’s policy framework is built to create a safe and efficient marketplace for both freelancers and clients.”

Proposed subsection: Historical change in pre-contract communication rules

The communication rules changed during the period relevant to this study. The User Agreement effective April 3, 2020 already prohibited unsolicited contact outside Upwork and required covered payments to remain on the platform during a 24-month non-circumvention period, subject to stated exceptions. The agreement effective June 26, 2020 added a separate Section 7.2 governing pre-contract communication. For interactions where the client held a Basic or Plus account, users were required to communicate solely through Upwork before entering a Service Contract and were prohibited from sharing, requesting or seeking direct contact details, except as otherwise provided on the Site. The restriction did not apply to interactions with an Enterprise Client as defined in the agreement. A violation was classified as a material breach and could result in permanent account suspension (Upwork, 2020a, §7.1; Upwork, 2020b, §7.2).

Pinpoints: April PDF p.18; June PDF pp.18–19. The pre-contract restriction is a documented addition, not evidence that all external contact had previously been unrestricted. Its after-contract expiry does not remove payment obligations.

M3 · Distinguish authorized sanctions from observed outcomes

Placement: Append to §5.1.4, p.22, without deleting its existing citation.

Locate: “The penalties for policy violations on Upwork range from warnings to account suspensions and permanent bans.”

Proposed addition

The historical agreement also identifies conduct for which a permanent suspension was expressly available. Section 7.2 of the June 26, 2020 User Agreement states that an account “may be permanently suspended” for violating the pre-contract communication restriction. This wording authorizes a possible sanction; it does not establish a required sequence of warnings or show how frequently the sanction was imposed. Assessing fairness in a particular decision requires the applicable policy version and a record of the conduct alleged and the platform’s response (Upwork, 2020b, §7.2).

Related check in §5.6, p.23: The recovered agreements do not validate the three narrated suspension, non-payment or mediation cases. Their dates and case identifiers remain unverified. Avoid using those cases as proof that the newly recovered clause was consistently enforced; retain them as attributed reports only if their cited sources support the individual narratives.

M4 · Make the fairness comparison conditional on the rule that applied

Placement: Append to §5.5, “Balancing Fairness,” p.23. Keep its existing paragraph and citation.

Locate: “Nonetheless, achieving perfect fairness is challenging, and Upwork continually updates its policies and processes based on user feedback (Wise, 2023).”

Proposed addition

The recovered June 2020 agreement identifies a difference in policy scope that should be separated from unequal enforcement. Its pre-contract communication restriction applied to interactions involving Basic or Plus client accounts, but not to interactions with Enterprise Clients. The definition included legacy Enterprise clients and Upwork Business Clients displaying the Enterprise badge on a job post or search tile. The relevant distinction was therefore the client-account category, rather than simply whether the user was a freelancer or a hiring company. This difference provides a basis for examining the justification and effects of account-tier rules, but it does not by itself demonstrate preferential handling of violations or appeals (Upwork, 2020b, §7.2).

Interpretive boundary: The exemption concerns §7.2 communication duties. It is not a blanket exemption from non-circumvention, payment rules or other policies. “Business customer” alone is too broad a label for the documented exception.

M5 · Replace the blanket description of off-platform transactions

Placement: Replace the body of §6.1.1, “Off-Platform Transactions,” p.24.

Locate: “This practice violates Upwork’s terms of service, as it deprives the platform of its commission and exposes both parties to potential payment and security risks.”

Proposed replacement

Off-platform arrangements have been discussed as a way for freelancers and clients to avoid platform service fees (Paterson, 2016). Their contractual status depends on the relationship and the applicable terms. In the recovered April and June 2020 User Agreements, payments arising from relationships formed through Upwork were generally required to remain on the platform for 24 months unless the relevant conversion provisions were satisfied. The agreements excluded relationships not formed through the platform, including parties who had worked together before meeting there. The June agreement separately regulated pre-contract communication for covered client-account categories. Moving payments off-platform in breach of these obligations should therefore be distinguished from a permitted conversion and from a communication violation (Upwork, 2020a, §§7.1–7.2; Upwork, 2020b, §§7.1–7.3).

Pinpoints: April PDF pp.18–19; June PDF pp.18–20. Paterson (2016) is retained from the manuscript; this mapping does not independently verify that source. No additional prevalence claim such as “one of the most common” is supplied by the recovered contracts. Renaming the wider §6 is optional; a contractual violation is not necessarily a loophole.

M6 · Date the fee claims in all three locations

Placement: Replace the freelancer fee bullet in the first Results summary, p.2, and the “High Fees” bullet in §4.8.2, p.21. Use the longer version below at p.21 and the shorter version at p.2.

Locate at p.21: “Upwork’s sliding fee scale (20% for the first $500 billed with a client, 10% for billings between $500.01 and $10,000, and 5% for billings above $10,000) is a point of contention (freelancermap GmbH, 2024).”

Proposed p.21 replacement

High Fees: Freelancers have criticized platform service fees as high, particularly for smaller projects (freelancermap GmbH, 2024). The recovered Fee and ACH Authorization Agreement effective October 23, 2019 documents marginal rates of 20% on the first $500 collected within a client relationship, 10% on the next $9,500, and 5% above $10,000, subject to specified alternative pricing provisions (Upwork, 2019a, §2.1). This historical schedule should not be treated as the general marketplace fee at the manuscript’s July 2024 date. Upwork reported replacing its tiered structure with a flat 10% fee in May 2023 for new contracts and existing contracts otherwise subject to 20%, while existing 5% contracts retained that rate through the end of 2023. Fee-related experiences should therefore be interpreted by policy period and contract status rather than pooled under a single schedule (Upwork Inc., 2024).

Proposed p.2 summary replacement

Freelancers: Reported concerns about high fees should be interpreted against the policy applicable to each experience. The recovered 2019 agreement documents the historical 20%/10%/5% tiers, while company disclosures identify the change to a flat 10% marketplace fee in May 2023 and the temporary retention of 5% on qualifying existing contracts through the end of that year. Secure payment and access to clients remained reported trade-offs (Upwork, 2019a; Upwork Inc., 2024).

Consistency edit at p.15, §4.1.1.3: In the Fiverr comparison, replace “which is higher than Upwork’s sliding fee scale” with “which should be compared with the Upwork schedule applicable to the same policy period.” Retain the existing Todorov (2023) citation; append the relevant Upwork reference if a numerical comparison is retained. This avoids independently revalidating Fiverr’s pricing within this limited update.

Evidence distinction: The archived fee agreement establishes the historical tiers, not their original 2016 introduction or their 2023 replacement. M7 · Give the “proposal machine” a contractual cost context

Placement: After the opening paragraph of §11.4, p.35; use a cross-reference rather than repeating the full addition in the p.3 Results summary.

Locate: “To consistently secure work, I found myself submitting dozens of proposals each day.”

Proposed addition

The proposal burden also has a policy context. The Freelancer Membership Agreement effective October 23, 2019 provided for Connects to submit proposals and allowed their purchase at the price advertised on the Site. It reserved the right to change membership fees, monthly Connects allowances and Connects prices on reasonable advance notice. High proposal volume should therefore be considered alongside the membership terms and Connects requirements applicable at the time. The agreement alone does not establish how many Connects I used or purchased, and the reported proposal and interview volumes are not a measure of actual expenditure (Upwork, 2019b, §2).

Related p.3 edit: Add “The applicable membership and Connects terms are discussed in §11.4” after the proposal-volume bullet. Do not infer a platform-wide success rate from the author's approximately 10% figure.

Limit: This agreement does not specify a fixed $0.15 token price, establish the first paid-Connects rollout date, or establish token requirements per application. Any historical spending calculation needs dated proposal and purchase records; do not multiply the narrative proposal count by an assumed token cost.
